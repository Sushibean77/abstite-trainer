Couinaud liver segmentsDiagram
  • Anterior view. Blue lines = hepatic veins (divide sectors vertically); dashed line = portal vein plane (divides superior from inferior segments).
  • Cantlie line (gallbladder fossa to IVC, along the middle hepatic vein) separates the right and left hemilivers.
  • Right hepatic vein: right posterior (VI, VII) vs right anterior (V, VIII). Falciform/umbilical fissure: IV vs II/III. Left hepatic vein runs between II and III.
  • Segment I (caudate): posterior, wraps the IVC, receives inflow from both sides and drains directly into the IVC (spared and hypertrophied in Budd-Chiari).
  • Right hepatectomy = V–VIII. Left hepatectomy = II–IV. Right trisectionectomy (extended right) = IV–VIII. Left lateral sectionectomy = II–III. Left trisectionectomy = II–IV + V, VIII.
  • Superior segments: II, IVa, VII, VIII. Inferior: III, IVb, V, VI. Gallbladder bed lies on IVb and V.
Strasberg bile duct injury classificationDiagram
  • A: leak from cystic duct stump or duct of Luschka, biliary tree in continuity. ERCP sphincterotomy/stent plus drainage.
  • B: aberrant right sectoral duct ligated (occluded). Often silent; later pain, cholangitis or segmental atrophy. Hepaticojejunostomy or resection only if symptomatic.
  • C: aberrant right sectoral duct transected, not ligated: leak not in continuity, so ERCP looks normal. Drain; usually needs hepaticojejunostomy.
  • D: lateral (partial) injury to a major duct. Small: primary repair over T-tube or ERCP stent. Large or thermal: treat as E.
  • E (Bismuth 1–5): complete transection or stricture of the hepatic duct. E1 >2 cm from confluence; E2 <2 cm; E3 at confluence, right and left ducts still communicate; E4 confluence destroyed, right and left separated; E5 = E plus aberrant right sectoral duct injury.
  • E injuries: Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy by an experienced HPB surgeon. Found intraoperatively without that expertise: place drains, do not attempt repair, transfer. Late presentation with sepsis: drain (percutaneous + PTC), define anatomy, repair at ~6–8 weeks.
  • Check for a concomitant right hepatic artery injury (most common associated vascular injury).
Hinchey classification (perforated diverticulitis)Diagram
  • I (modified: Ia phlegmon, Ib pericolic abscess) and II: antibiotics; percutaneous drainage for abscesses ≥3–4 cm. Colonoscopy ~6–8 weeks after complicated disease.
  • Uncomplicated diverticulitis (no abscess/perforation) in selected immunocompetent patients can be managed without antibiotics.
  • III: sigmoid resection. Hemodynamically stable: primary anastomosis ± diverting loop ileostomy is acceptable and gives higher stoma-free survival than Hartmann (LADIES, DIVERTI). Laparoscopic lavage alone is not preferred (more reoperation).
  • IV or unstable/immunosuppressed patient: Hartmann procedure.
  • Elective resection after recovery is individualized, no longer based on number of episodes; take the distal margin to proximal rectum (where taeniae splay).
Groin: laparoscopic view, hernia sites, triangles of doom and painDiagram
  • Right groin seen from inside the abdomen (TAPP/TEP view). Inferior epigastric vessels are the landmark: direct hernia medial, indirect lateral (through the deep ring), femoral below the iliopubic tract and medial to the femoral vein.
  • Hesselbach triangle (direct): inferior epigastric vessels (lateral), lateral border of rectus (medial), inguinal ligament (inferior).
  • Triangle of doom: vas deferens (medial), gonadal vessels (lateral). Contains external iliac artery and vein. No tacks.
  • Triangle of pain: gonadal vessels (medial), iliopubic tract (superior). Contains lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, femoral branch of genitofemoral nerve, femoral nerve. No tacks below the iliopubic tract lateral to the gonadals.
  • Corona mortis: obturator to external iliac/inferior epigastric vascular connection crossing Cooper ligament; bleeds when fixing mesh to Cooper.
  • Open repair nerves: ilioinguinal (on the cord, most commonly injured), iliohypogastric, genital branch of genitofemoral. Femoral canal borders: inguinal ligament (anterior), Cooper (posterior), lacunar ligament (medial), femoral vein (lateral).
Retroperitoneal hematoma zonesDiagram
  • Zone 1 (central): explore all, blunt or penetrating. Supramesocolic: left medial visceral rotation (Mattox) exposes the aorta. Inframesocolic/IVC and right-sided: right medial visceral rotation (Cattell-Braasch) plus Kocher.
  • Zone 2 (lateral/perinephric): penetrating → explore (selective if staged by CT and stable). Blunt → do not explore unless expanding, pulsatile, or there is urine leak/colon injury.
  • Zone 3 (pelvic): penetrating → explore. Blunt → do not open; pelvic binder, preperitoneal packing, angioembolization or REBOA zone 3.
  • Retrohepatic hematoma that is contained: leave alone, pack.
Goodsall rule (anal fistula)Diagram
  • External opening anterior to the transverse line: straight radial tract to the nearest crypt.
  • External opening posterior to the line: curved tract to the posterior midline.
  • Exception: anterior opening >3 cm from the anal verge may also track to the posterior midline (dashed).
  • Intersphincteric (most common) and low transsphincteric: fistulotomy. High transsphincteric, anterior in women, Crohn or poor continence: seton, LIFT or advancement flap. Crohn: draining seton plus anti-TNF.
  • Parks types: intersphincteric, transsphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric.
Neck zones (penetrating trauma)Diagram
  • Current "no-zone" approach: hard signs (expanding/pulsatile hematoma, active bleeding, shock, air bubbling, stridor, massive hemoptysis/hematemesis) → airway and operative exploration regardless of zone.
  • No hard signs and platysma violated: CT angiography of the neck; further esophagram/endoscopy/bronchoscopy as directed.
  • Zone I: proximal control may need sternotomy (or left anterolateral thoracotomy for proximal left subclavian). Zone III: distal control is difficult; angiographic approaches useful. Zone II: most accessible; incision along anterior border of SCM.
  • Esophageal injury is the most commonly missed; delayed diagnosis drives mortality.
Rule of nines (TBSA)Diagram
  • Count only partial- and full-thickness burns (not superficial/first degree). Patient's palm including fingers ≈ 1% TBSA.
  • Children: head relatively larger, legs smaller; use Lund-Browder for accuracy. Each year of age moves ~1% from head to legs.
  • Formal fluid resuscitation: ≥20% TBSA adults (≥10–15% children). ATLS start: 2 mL/kg/%TBSA lactated Ringer (3 mL/kg in children <30 kg, 4 mL/kg for electrical), half in first 8 h from time of burn, then titrate to urine output 0.5 mL/kg/h adults, 1 mL/kg/h children.
